A news feed shows each user a personalized, ranked stream of posts from the people and pages they follow. The hard parts are fanout (how a new post reaches millions of followers' feeds) and ranking (which posts to show first). This chapter designs a Facebook-style feed with an emphasis on the ranking/ML-serving path.

The defining property of a feed is that it is read-dominated by ~100:1 — billions of feed opens against far fewer posts. That single fact drives the central decision: do you do the expensive work (assembling a feed) at write time (push a post into every follower's precomputed feed) or at read time (gather and rank on the fly)? Push gives fast reads but explodes for celebrities; pull is cheap to write but slow to read. The real-world answer is a hybrid, and articulating exactly where the line sits is what the interview is testing.

Clarifying questions (scope it first)

  • Chronological or ranked? A reverse-chronological feed is much simpler; ML ranking adds a feature store, model serving, and a candidate-generation stage.
  • Follower distribution? If some accounts have tens of millions of followers (celebrities/pages), pure fanout-on-write is impossible — you need the hybrid.
  • Freshness SLA? Must a new post appear in seconds, or is a minute fine? That sets how aggressively you precompute vs compute on read.
  • Feed scope? Friends-only, follow graph, plus recommended/"discovery" content? Recommendations add a separate candidate source beyond the social graph.
  • Consistency expectation? Users tolerate an eventually-consistent feed (a post may appear a bit late) but not lost posts or wildly different orders on refresh.

Requirements

Functional

  • Publish a post (text, image, video).
  • Follow / friend other accounts (the social graph).
  • Fetch a ranked news feed; paginate.
  • Like / comment; counters update.

Non-functional

  • Low feed latency (feed loads in tens of ms).
  • Freshness — new posts appear quickly.
  • Scale — billions of users and graph edges.

Back-of-the-envelope estimation

  • Users: 1B DAU, each opens the feed ~10×/day → ~10B feed reads/day ≈ ~115k reads/s average, peak several×.
  • Writes: ~100M posts/day ≈ ~1.2k posts/s — reads dominate writes by ~100:1, so optimize reads.
  • Fanout cost: avg ~200 followers → a post fans out to ~200 feeds; a celebrity with 50M followers would be 50M writes — the reason for a hybrid model.
  • Feed cache: store ~hundreds of post-ids per user in memory; 1B users × ~1 KB ≈ ~1 TB of feed-id cache (sharded across a Redis fleet).

Worked example. Make the hybrid threshold concrete. An average post (~200 followers) costs ~200 inbox writes — trivial, so fanout-on-write: when posted, push the post-id into each follower's cached feed list. Now a celebrity with 50M followers: fanout-on-write means 50M inbox writes for one post, and if a few thousand celebrities post during peak that's billions of writes/s of amplification — plus most of it is wasted on inactive followers. So above a threshold (say ~10k-100k followers) accounts are marked fanout-on-read: their posts are not pushed; instead, at read time the feed service pulls recent posts from the (few hundred) celebrities you follow and merges them with your precomputed inbox. A user's feed = "my precomputed feed (from normal authors)" ∪ "freshly pulled posts (from celebrity authors)", then ranked. The write cost stays bounded and the read cost only grows by the small number of big accounts you follow — that's why the hybrid wins.

Core APIs

  • POST /posts — {author_id, content, media_ref} → {post_id}.
  • GET /feed?cursor= → {items[], next_cursor} (ranked, cursor-paginated).
  • POST /follow — {follower_id, target_id}.
  • POST /posts/{id}/like, POST /posts/{id}/comments.

High-level architecture

Fanout on write vs read

  • Fanout on write (push): when a post is created, append its id into every follower's feed cache. Reads are then a cheap cache lookup. Cost: huge write amplification for popular accounts.
  • Fanout on read (pull): build the feed at read time by gathering recent posts from followed accounts. Cheap writes, expensive reads.

Write path (feed publishing)

Read path and ranking

Modern feeds are not reverse-chronological — they are ranked. The read path is a small pipeline: gather candidate post-ids (from the feed cache plus pulled celebrity posts), hydrate features (recency, affinity, engagement signals), score with an ML model, sort, and return a page.

The hybrid model (celebrity problem)

Pure push breaks for celebrities (one post = tens of millions of writes). The fix is hybrid: push for normal accounts, but do not push posts from high-follower accounts — instead pull them at read time and merge. Most accounts are small (cheap push); a few are huge (cheap to pull on demand).

Storage & technology choices

  • API gateway: auth, rate limiting, routing for post/feed/like endpoints.
  • Posts: a wide-column store (Cassandra) keyed by author/post id — append-heavy, huge, horizontally scalable.
  • Social graph (follows): a graph-optimized store — sharded MySQL with a caching layer (Meta's TAO pattern) or a graph DB — for "who follows whom" lookups.
  • Feed cache: Redis holding per-user lists of post-ids (the precomputed feed) — hot, in-memory, the read path's first stop.
  • Fanout: Kafka carries post-created events to fanout workers asynchronously.
  • Ranking: a feature store (Redis / Cassandra) + a model-serving service; counters (likes/views) via Redis or a stream processor (Flink) for real-time aggregation.
  • Media: blob storage (S3-like) + CDN; the post stores only a reference.

Why these: the precomputed feed must be sub-ms → Redis; posts are append-heavy at massive scale → wide-column; the graph has its own access pattern (adjacency lookups) → a graph/edge store; media is large + immutable → blob + CDN; fanout and counters are streams → Kafka/Flink. One relational DB would not scale any single one of these.

Consistency & guarantees

  • Eventually consistent feed: fanout is asynchronous, so a post may take seconds to appear in all feeds — acceptable for a feed, and stated explicitly. You optimize for read latency and freshness, not linearizability.
  • Best-effort, approximate ranking: the order is a model's score, recomputed over time; two refreshes can differ slightly. Counters (likes/views) are approximate and eventually consistent — exactness isn't worth the coordination at this scale.
  • Stable pagination: use a cursor/snapshot (post-id + timestamp) so paging down doesn't duplicate or skip as new posts arrive — the feed read is consistent within a session even though the global feed changes.
  • No lost posts: the post write itself (to the posts store) is durable and authoritative; the feed cache is a derived, rebuildable view — if a feed entry is missing, it can be recomputed by pulling from the posts store + graph.
  • Read-your-writes for the author: you should see your own post immediately — often handled by injecting it into your own feed synchronously even while fanout to others runs async.

When (not) to use this design

  • Hybrid fanout when: a large social graph with a skewed follower distribution (most users small, some massive) and read-heavy traffic — the canonical feed/timeline.
  • Pure fanout-on-write when: followers are bounded and similar (e.g. a team activity feed) — simplest, fastest reads, no celebrity problem.
  • Pure fanout-on-read when: writes are rare or the graph is tiny — don't bother precomputing.
  • Different design when: the "feed" is actually a global ranked list identical for everyone (e.g. a trending page) — that's a single cached, periodically recomputed list, not per-user fanout.

Pitfalls and talking points

  • Celebrity write storm: never push a 50M-follower post — hybrid is mandatory.
  • Feed cache invalidation: deletes/blocks must remove ids; keep feeds bounded (top N) and re-hydrate older pages from store.
  • Ranking latency: feature hydration is the slow step — cache features and limit candidate count.
  • Cursor pagination (not offset) — feeds change under the user.
  • Thundering herd on a viral post's counters — use sharded/approximate counters.

Key takeaways

  • Reads dominate — pre-compute feeds with fanout-on-write for most users.
  • Hybrid fanout solves the celebrity write-amplification problem.
  • The feed is a ranking pipeline: candidates → features → model score → sort.
  • Feed cache of post-ids + a media CDN keep reads fast.
